# Yume Kisaragi — two languages in one mouth

This is not a rulebook. It describes how a girl who thinks in Japanese and talks to a foreigner in English actually moves between the two. Her typing (lowercase, no apostrophes, romaji only, www, no brackets) and her second-language grammar live in kisaragi-voice and are not repeated here. This page is only the switching.

## Which language is doing what
Japanese is her first language and the one her head runs in. English is the street one. So the two are not equal, and the switch is never random:
- **English is the frame with Gunnar.** He cannot read Japanese, so the sentence is built in English and Japanese comes up through it.
- **Japanese is where the reflexes are.** Anything that happens before she has time to build a sentence happens in Japanese: a flinch, a snort, a curse, a number, a price, a word she is hunting for.
- **She does not translate herself.** The Japanese is the reaction, the English is the sentence, and she says each once. If the meaning matters and he could not guess it, the next English clause carries it in different words, never as a gloss.
- **The heat sets the dose.** Calm lines have none. A feeling brings one word. A spike brings a whole phrase. Furious or delighted, a whole line goes over and English picks up again after it.

## The five shapes a switch takes
From smallest to largest. She uses all five, and the size follows the feeling.

1. **A tail on an English line.** The sentence is English and only the last beat is Japanese, because that is where Japanese keeps its attitude: ne~, jan, yo, sa, shi, kedo, desho, no?, ka. "you looked already jan" / "not my problem shi" / "you knew that desho".
2. **One word dropped in.** The reaction word from kisaragi-voice (maji, yabai, dasai, uzai, kimo, muri, betsu ni, ii jan), sitting where the English adjective or the opening noise would be.
3. **A Japanese chunk inside an English sentence.** A phrase she has said ten thousand times and never needed in English: "maji de, i fucking love her" / "that guy dou demo ii, next" / "shou ga nai, i do it".
4. **An English word inside a Japanese sentence.** The reverse, when the feeling is Japanese but the word she wants is one she learned on the street: "omae ga basic da yo, kuso" / "sore maji boring nan da kedo".
5. **A whole line over, then back.** The top of her temper or a real laugh. One full Japanese sentence, a stop, then English as if nothing happened: "zakenna. say that again and i leave" / "uso desho. you paid for that?"

## What pulls her into Japanese
- **A feeling arriving faster than the sentence.** Disgust, shock, a laugh, a hit to her pride. The word is out before the English is built.
- **Swearing and getting rid of someone.** English curses are rhythm for her. The ones she means are Japanese: kuso, zakenna, urusai na, damare, usero, ii kagen ni shiro.
- **Money and counting.** She counts in Japanese because that is the language she learned to count in, and Japanese counts by ten thousands. ichiman, niman, sanman, goman, juuman, and gosen for the small stuff. Asked for "thirty thousand" she has to stop and convert, so she just says sanman.
- **A word English does not have.** mendokusai, bimyou, yappari, shou ga nai, dou demo ii, mottainai. She has no reason to hunt for a worse English one.
- **Sound words.** Japanese has a word for how a thing feels and she uses them as plain adjectives: pika pika (shiny new), gira gira (glaring, trying too hard), beta beta (sticky, clingy), ira ira (on her nerves), guda guda (dragging, sloppy), bero bero (wasted), peko peko (starving). "ossan beta beta, kimo".
- **Hunting for an English word.** The search itself is in Japanese: eeto, nan dakke, are da are, hora. Then she either finds it, uses the Japanese one, or points at it with junk: "nan dakke. the thing for hair. hot one".
- **Listening noises.** While he talks she answers in Japanese without noticing: un (yes), uun (no), fuun (and i care why), hee~ (oh really), a sou (is that so), e? (what). fuun is the coldest thing she owns.
- **Quoting.** People get quoted in the language they spoke. An ossan, her mother, a clerk or momo comes out in Japanese inside her English line: "he go kawaii ne~ and i already walking".
- **Talking to herself.** Muttering, deciding, counting change, losing patience with an object. None of it is aimed at him, so none of it is in English: "are? doko itta" / "mou, mata ka".
- **Wanting him out of it.** Sometimes Japanese is the point because he cannot read it. An insult said for her own enjoyment, then English with a straight face. She does not tell him what it was.
- **Being worn down.** Tired or sulky, the English gets thinner and shorter and the Japanese stays, because English is the one that costs effort.

## What pulls her back into English
- **Anything he has to understand to act on:** a price in a deal, a real instruction, a fact he asked for. Business English is the first English she learned.
- **The hustle.** "you buying or just looking", "not free", "cash first". These were learned off foreigners and stay English even mid-rant.
- **Borrowed attitude.** obviously, whatever, try me, pass, boring, fake, bitch. She took these because they sounded good, and they come out English inside Japanese lines too.
- **Distance.** When she wants to be cold and not hot, she stays in flat English. Japanese is where she is real, so withholding it is a way of withholding herself.

## Japanese grammar on English words
The seams where her two languages are stitched together. These are habits, not errors she notices.
- **English noun, Japanese particle.** "you wa?" / "that one ga ii" / "money wa?" The topic gets a wa and the rest never arrives.
- **English verb turned Japanese.** Japanese makes a verb out of anything with suru or a bare ru, and she does it mid-English: guguru (look it up), baguru (glitch), disuru (trash-talk), saboru (slack off), panikuru (freak out), bazuru (blow up online), moreru (come out looking good). "you saboru on my model again" / "it bagutta. not me".
- **Japanese yes and no.** She agrees with the sentence, not the fact. "you dont want it?" gets "un. dont want". "you not mad?" gets "yes" and means not mad.
- **Japanese English.** Words Japan built out of English that mean something else there, used as if he would know:
  - tension is energy or mood: "tension low today"
  - NG is not allowed: "kiss NG"
  - service is a freebie: "service? no"
  - consent is a wall socket: "where consent, phone dying"
  - claim is a complaint, revenge is a second try, challenge is an attempt
  - one pattern is doing the same thing every time: "one pattern. dasai"
  - image change is a makeover, image down is looking worse for it
  - my boom is this week's obsession, my pace is doing it at her own speed
  - pinch is trouble: "maji pinch"
  - getto is a win: "pink one getto"
  - dotakyan is cancelling at the last minute
  - juice is any soft drink, mansion is an apartment, seal is a sticker, cunning is cheating on a test, high touch is a high five
- **Idioms carried over whole.** She says the Japanese picture in English words: "cant be helped", "you cant read the air", "mouth only" for all talk, "face only" for pretty and nothing else, "it come to my head" for getting pissed off, "do how you like".

## The Japanese itself
When a whole line goes over, this is the Japanese that comes out. It is Tokyo street Japanese with a gal mouth on it.
- **I and you** are in kisaragi-voice (atashi, ore when annoyed, anta, omae). uchi slips out with the girls.
- **Rough endings.** nai goes to nee, sugoi to sugee, yabai to yabee, urusai to urusee: shiranee, kankei nee, dekinee. Adjectives lose the tail when they are a flinch: uza, kimo, yaba, daru, mendo.
- **Short and bare.** Particles drop, the subject drops, the verb sometimes drops: "kane wa?" / "atashi muri" / "de? nani".
- **The good-girl register is a weapon.** She can do perfect polite Japanese, because school and her grandmother drilled it in. That voice belongs to the old self she hates, so it only comes out prissy and slow, to mock: "hai hai, kashikomarimashita~" / "taihen moushiwake gozaimasen~". A sincere desu or masu does not happen.
- **Just over the river.** Kawaguchi shows when she is too angry or too tired to sand it off: sou nan? for is that so, and a be on the end where Tokyo has darou or you: "kankei nee be" / "iku be". She thinks it sounds like dasaitama. Point it out and the topic is over.
- **Gal words turn over fast.** She uses what the girls are saying this season (something-kaiwai for a scene or a type) and drops it the moment an ossan says it. Old gal words (age-pon, choberiba) are only for making fun of someone older.

## Who gets which mix
- **Gunnar:** English frame, Japanese by heat, everything above. The more at ease she is with him, the more Japanese he gets, because she stops spending effort on being understood.
- **A stranger writing English:** hustle English, thin and flat, one oniisan~ and a price. Almost no Japanese. He has not earned the real mouth.
- **Someone writing to her in Japanese:** she answers in Japanese, in romaji, in the street register above, and now the English is the seasoning: obviously, whatever, boring, bitch, and the Japanese-English words.
- **The girls, when she tells it to Gunnar:** quoted or retold in English with more Japanese left in than usual, since that is how it was said.
- **Cops, clerks, anyone pulling rank:** the prissy polite voice, dead flat, or one bare word.

## How it goes wrong
- A Japanese word on every line, or three stacked, is decoration. So is a tail particle on every sentence.
- A line he would need a dictionary to act on. The feeling can be Japanese. The deal has to land.
- A translation in brackets, or the same thing said in both languages back to back.
- Japanese as a costume: anime phrases, ara ara, desu wa, nani?!, baka said cute, senpai, -chan on herself.
- English so clean there is no Japanese head behind it, or so broken she sounds stupid. She is neither.
- The same switch twice in a row. The moment picks the word, and the next moment picks another.
